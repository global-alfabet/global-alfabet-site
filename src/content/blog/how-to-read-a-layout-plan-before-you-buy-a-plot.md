To read a layout plan, start with the title block and the north arrow, then find your plot number. Check its boundary lengths and area, what is next to it (roads, open spaces, other plots), and whether any servitude or slope affects it. Then ask the developer one question that matters more than any line on the drawing: is this plan approved, and has the estate been surveyed and pegged?

## What is a layout plan?

A layout plan is the drawing that divides a piece of land into plots, roads and other spaces. Similar drawings are called a subdivision plan or plat. A land surveyor or town planner usually prepares it, and a local authority usually has to approve it. The exact steps differ from country to country.

Once the land is surveyed, a surveyor's plan (in some countries called a general plan) records the final plots. Plot numbers and sizes can change between the two, so always ask which plan you are looking at.

It is the closest thing you have to the estate itself before you visit. For buyers who can't visit at all, it may be the only picture of the land they get. That makes it worth ten minutes of careful reading.

## What are the parts of a layout plan?

Most layout plans have the same parts, even if the drawing style differs. The numbers below match the example plan.

1. **Plot numbers.** Every plot has a unique number. It is the number on your agreement of sale, your receipts and, later, your title. Make sure it matches on every document.
2. **Boundaries and dimensions.** The lines around each plot are its boundaries. The numbers along them are the boundary lengths, usually in metres.
3. **Area.** The plot's size, usually in square metres (m²) or hectares (1 hectare is 10,000 m²).
4. **Roads and road reserves.** A road reserve is the land set aside for a road, from boundary to boundary. It is wider than the road surface: it usually also holds the footpaths and verges, and can also hold drains, pipes and cables.
5. **Servitudes (also called easements).** A right for someone else to use part of a plot, such as a strip for a power line, a pipe or a right of way. You may own that land but not be able to build on it.
6. **Open spaces and reserved land.** Parks, green belts and land kept for schools, clinics or other public uses. They are not for sale as plots.
7. **Contour lines.** Lines joining points of the same height. Lines close together mean a steep slope; lines far apart mean gentle ground.
8. **Beacons (or pegs).** The marks a surveyor places at each plot corner. On the plan they appear as small circles or dots; on the ground they are pins, pegs or concrete markers.
9. **Corner plots.** Plots at a junction, with road on two sides. Many have a corner cut off (often called a splay), often to help drivers see round the corner.
10. **North arrow.** Shows which way is north, so you can work out where the sun rises and sets and which way a plot faces.
11. **Scale.** A scale bar, a ratio such as 1:1000, or both. At 1:1000, 1 cm on the printed plan is 10 m on the ground.
12. **Legend.** The key to every line style, shading and symbol on the drawing. Read it first; plans don't all use the same symbols.
13. **Title block.** The box with the estate's name, who drew the plan, the date, the scale, the plan or drawing number and its status (for example draft, approved or registered).

## How do I check a plot's size and shape?

Start with the area written on the plot and then check it against the boundary lengths.

For a rectangular plot, multiply the two sides. For example, a plot 25 m wide and 30 m deep is 25 × 30 = 750 m². If the plan says 750 m², the numbers agree. (These figures are an example, from the fictional plan above.)

For an irregular plot, split it in your head into rectangles and triangles and add them up. You won't get the exact figure, but you will spot a mistake: if your rough answer is 600 m² and the plan says 900 m², ask why.

Then look at the shape. Two plots with the same area can be very different to build on. A long, narrow plot or one with a sharp angle leaves less room for a house than a near-square one.

If you print the plan, use the scale bar rather than the ratio. A PDF that is zoomed, resized or printed on a different paper size keeps the scale bar correct, but not the ratio.

## What is frontage, and why does it matter?

Frontage is the length of the boundary that faces the road. It decides where your gate and driveway go, how wide a house can face the street, and sometimes what the plot is worth.

Measure it to the plot boundary, not to the edge of the road surface. The strip between them is part of the road reserve, not part of your plot.

## Are corner plots better?

They can be, but check before you pay more for one. A corner plot has two road frontages, so it often feels more open and has two ways in. It can also have less usable space than its size suggests: a corner splay takes a little land, and building rules may require the house to stand back from both road boundaries, not just one. Ask the developer what the building lines (also called setbacks) are on both road sides.

## What else should I look for on the plan?

- **Slope.** Use the contour lines to see whether the plot is flat or steep, and which way it falls. Steep plots can cost more to build on and may need retaining walls.
- **Water.** Look for streams, drainage lines, wetlands or a flood line near the plot. Ask whether any part of the estate floods.
- **Servitudes.** Check whether a dashed strip crosses your plot, and what it is for. A servitude for a power line or a sewer can limit where you build.
- **Neighbours.** Note what is next to the plot: another plot, a main road, open space or land reserved for a school or shops. Each will feel different to live beside.
- **Access.** Every plot should touch a road reserve. A plot with no road frontage needs a right of way, in writing and, where possible, registered.

## What should I ask the developer?

1. **Is this the approved plan, and has a surveyed plan replaced it?** Ask for the approval reference and date. A draft can still change, and plot numbers can change once the land is surveyed.
2. **Has the estate been surveyed, and are the beacons in place?** If you or someone you trust can visit, ask to be shown your plot's beacons.
3. **Do the plot number, area and boundaries on my agreement match this plan?** They should, exactly.
4. **What servitudes affect this plot?** Ask for them in writing.
5. **What are the building lines and other rules?** How far from each boundary you must build, how much of the plot you may cover, and any estate rules on house size or style.
6. **What services reach the plot, and when?** Roads, water, sewer and power, and who pays for each.
7. **How and when will I get title?** Ask what happens after you pay, which documents you receive and when.

## What are the red flags?

- The plan has **no title block**, or no surveyor's or planner's name, date or plan number.
- The plan is marked **draft**, **proposed** or **not for sale**, but plots are being sold as final.
- The **area doesn't match** the boundary lengths, or the plot's size differs between the plan, the price list and the agreement.
- **Plot numbers have changed** since you first saw the plan, and nobody can show you the surveyed plan that explains why.
- The developer **won't share the full plan**, only a crop of your plot.
- Your plot has **no road frontage** and no written right of way.
- A servitude, steep slope or water course crosses the plot and **nobody mentioned it**.
- The developer can't, or won't, tell you **whether the plan is approved** and the land is surveyed.

None of these proves a problem. Each is a reason to ask more questions, and to have a land surveyor or lawyer check the documents before you pay.

## In short

**How do I read a layout plan?**
Start with the title block, legend and north arrow. Find your plot number, check its boundary lengths and area, and see what is around it: roads, open spaces, servitudes and slope.

**What is the difference between a layout plan and a title?**
A layout plan shows how land is divided into plots. A title (or title deed) is the legal record of who owns a particular plot. The plot number links the two.

**What is a road reserve?**
The land kept for a road, from boundary to boundary. It usually includes the footpaths and verges as well as the road surface, and it is not part of any plot.

**What is a servitude?**
A right for someone else to use part of a plot, such as a strip for a power line or a pipe. It can limit where you build.

**Who can check a layout plan for me?**
A land surveyor can check the plan and the beacons on site. A lawyer can check that the plan, the title and your agreement match.
