A coffee shop website needs to answer four questions in seconds, on a phone: are you open now, where are you, what's on the menu, and what does it cost? Then come ordering (if you offer it), real photos, events, easy access and a fast page, with the same details on your Google Business Profile.

The examples below come from Pockwell, a concept in our Studio library: a website and order-ahead app for a neighbourhood coffee shop. It is concept work for a fictional brand, designed to show each idea clearly. Pockwell is not a real business, and its prices are examples.

## 1. Opening hours, on the first screen

Someone looking up a coffee shop usually wants a coffee soon. The first thing they need to know is whether you are open.

- **Put today's hours near the top of the home page,** not only on a contact page.
- **Plan for holidays.** Add a line when your hours change for a holiday or an event, and take it down after.
- **Match your Google Business Profile.** Google lets you set [special hours for holidays and events](https://support.google.com/business/answer/6303076?hl=en) without changing your regular hours, and recommends confirming your hours for official holidays even when they don't change.

See it on [Pockwell](/studio/library/pockwell): its Find us screen shows weekday, Saturday and Sunday hours in one small card, next to the map.

## 2. Where you are, and directions in one tap

Your address is the second question. Make it easy to read and easy to use.

- **Write the full address as text,** plus one line that helps on the street: "Corner of the high street and the station road", "Next to the bookshop", "Entrance on the side street".
- **Add a Get directions button** that opens the visitor's map app. Google's [Maps URLs](https://developers.google.com/maps/documentation/urls/get-started) build a universal link that opens Google Maps on phones and computers, and they don't need an API key.
- **Say what's useful nearby,** such as parking or the nearest bus stop.

## 3. A menu with prices, written as text

Next comes the menu. Three rules make it work.

- **Use real text, not a photo or a PDF.** A photo of a chalkboard is hard to read on a phone and can't be resized. The W3C's accessibility guidance says [real text styled with CSS is better than images of text](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/textual/): it can be resized without losing clarity, and readers can change its colours. Search engines can read it too.
- **Show prices.** A menu without prices leaves people guessing. If prices change often, show the main drinks and food and keep them right.
- **Keep it short and current.** Group it simply, say which milks you offer, and remove what you no longer serve.

Then point Google to it. Food and drink businesses can add a [menu link and menu items to their Business Profile](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9455840?hl=en). If your site has structured data, Google's [LocalBusiness guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/local-business) lists `menu`, the address of your menu, as a recommended property for food establishments.

See it on [Pockwell](/studio/library/pockwell): its menu screen has tabs for coffee, tea and pastries, and every item shows a name, one line and a price (labelled as example prices).

## 4. Built for the phone first

Many of the questions above are asked on a phone, often on the way. Design for that screen first. Pick one main button ("Order ahead", "See the menu" or "Get directions") and keep it in reach, make phone numbers and emails tappable links, and make buttons big enough for a thumb. Our [5 checks for a small business website](/blog/small-business-website-checklist) covers these in more detail.

## 5. Ordering ahead, only if you really offer it

Order ahead saves people time on busy mornings. But a button that promises more than you do causes more trouble than no button at all.

- **If you take orders,** say plainly how it works: how to order, roughly how long it takes, and how people pay.
- **If you don't,** say so in a friendly way ("Walk in, we're quick") and leave the button out.

See it on [Pockwell](/studio/library/pockwell): the order screen shows the drinks, an estimated pickup time and one line, "Pay when you collect", so the visitor knows what happens next.

## 6. Photos of your real place

People choose a coffee shop partly for how it feels. Show them.

- **Use your own photos:** the counter, the seats by the window, a drink as you serve it, the front door so people recognise it from the street.
- **Avoid stock photos of other cafés.** They say nothing about your place.

Google says photos and videos on your Business Profile [show customers what you offer and tell the story of your business](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en). The same photos can work on your site.

## 7. Events and news, with dates

Live music, tastings or a new seasonal drink give people a reason to come back. Give every event a date and time, keep events in one place (such as a short "This week" section), and take each one down when it's over: an old event makes a shop look closed. Post it on Google too: Business Profile [event posts need a title and start and end dates](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7342169?hl=en).

## 8. Easy for everyone to use

An accessible coffee shop website helps every visitor, not only people with a disability.

- **Strong contrast.** The W3C's WCAG 2.2 guideline asks for a [contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text, and 3:1 for large text](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum).
- **Say what visitors need to know:** whether the entrance is step-free, whether there is an accessible toilet, and whether dogs are welcome.
- **Describe your images** with short alt text: "Window seats, with the counter behind", not "image1".

## 9. Fast on mobile data

A coffee shop page should load fast on a phone with a weak signal. Put your address into [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev). Google's web.dev counts a [Largest Contentful Paint of 2.5 seconds or less](https://web.dev/articles/vitals) as good: that is how long the main content takes to appear.

Start with the photos: resize each to the size it is shown at and save it in a modern format such as WebP. Then remove autoplay videos, big sliders and widgets you don't use.

## 10. The same details everywhere

Your website, your Google Business Profile and your social media pages should say the same thing: the same hours, address, phone number and menu link. Google says [businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up in local search results](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en).

Whenever your hours, prices or menu change, update both on the same day.

## Your checklist

Open your website on your phone, on mobile data, and check:

1. Today's hours are on the first screen, and holiday hours are up to date.
2. The address is written as text, with a Get directions button that opens a map app.
3. The menu is real text, with current prices.
4. One main button, and every phone number and email opens in one tap.
5. Ordering ahead is explained plainly, or the button isn't there.
6. The photos show your real place, as it looks today.
7. Every event has a date, and past events are gone.
8. Text is easy to read, and the page says whether the entrance is step-free.
9. PageSpeed Insights shows the main page loading in 2.5 seconds or less, or you know what to fix first.
10. Hours, address and menu match your Google Business Profile.

## In short

**What should a coffee shop website include?**
Opening hours, the address with a directions link, a menu with prices as text, one clear button, real photos, dated events, access details and fast pages.

**Should a coffee shop menu be a PDF?**
It's better as text on the page. Text can be resized and read on any phone. Keep a PDF only as an extra for people who want to print it.

**How do I keep my website and Google listing in step?**
Change both on the same day, every time your hours, prices or menu change. Use special hours on your Google Business Profile for holidays.
