Before you buy a plot, check five things: that the seller has the right to sell it, that the plot on paper is the plot on the ground, that roads, water and power reach it (or when they will), what the rules let you build, and that every payment term is written down. Ask for documents, not promises, and have a lawyer or conveyancer check them before you pay a deposit. Most problems with land are cheap to find before you pay and expensive to fix after.

## Why these checks matter

A plot is often the largest thing a person buys. Unlike most things you buy, you can't return it, and many buyers pay months before they can build. Some never visit the land before they pay, because they live far away.

The checks below are the same whether you buy from a developer selling a new estate or from a private seller. Some markets call a plot a stand, a lot or an erf; the checks don't change.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. The law on buying land differs from country to country, and sometimes from one area to the next. Use it to know what to ask for, then have a qualified professional check the answers where you are buying.

## 1. Who owns the land, and can they sell it?

The first question is not about the plot. It is about the seller.

- **Ask for the ownership document.** In many places this is a title deed or a certificate of title, recorded in a land register. Ask for a copy, and ask whose name is on it.
- **Check the name matches the seller.** If a developer is selling, the land may be in the name of a company. Ask how the seller is connected to the owner, and for proof that they may sell on the owner's behalf.
- **Ask whether the plot has its own title yet.** On a new estate, the whole piece of land often has one title until it is subdivided and each plot is registered. Ask what you are buying today (a plot with its own title, or the right to one later) and when you will get the title in your name.
- **Ask about loans and claims on the land.** Land can be held as security for a loan (a mortgage or bond), or have other claims against it. Ask the seller in writing, and ask your lawyer to check the register.
- **Have the register checked independently.** Don't rely on a copy the seller gives you. A lawyer or conveyancer can search the official record and tell you who owns the land and what is registered against it.

If the seller can't show who owns the land, stop there until they can.

## 2. Is the plot on paper the plot on the ground?

The plot number, size and shape on your agreement should match the plan and the land itself.

- **Get the layout plan.** Ask for the full plan, not a crop of your plot, and ask whether it is approved. Our guide on [how to read a layout plan before you buy a plot](/blog/how-to-read-a-layout-plan-before-you-buy-a-plot) explains each part of it.
- **Check the size against the sides.** The plan shows each boundary's length and the plot's area. For a rectangle, multiply the two sides; for other shapes, a rough estimate will still catch a big mistake.
- **Ask whether the land has been surveyed and pegged.** A surveyor marks each plot corner with a peg or beacon. Ask to be shown your plot's pegs. If you can't visit, ask someone you trust to go, or ask for recent, dated photos of each peg.
- **Look for anything crossing the plot.** A servitude (also called an easement) for a power line, pipe or right of way can limit where you build. Ask for any that affect your plot in writing.
- **Check the plot number is the same everywhere.** On the plan, the price list, the agreement and your receipts. A changed number needs an explanation and an updated plan.

If something doesn't add up, a land surveyor can check the plan and the pegs on site.

## 3. Can you reach it, and do services reach it?

A plot is only as useful as the road to it and the services that reach it.

- **Access.** Every plot should touch a public road or have a written right of way. Ask who builds and maintains the estate roads, and whether they are finished.
- **Water.** Is there a mains connection, or will you need a borehole or a tank? If it is planned, ask who pays for it and when it is due.
- **Power.** Is there an electricity connection at the plot boundary, or only somewhere in the estate? Ask about the cost of connecting.
- **Sewer and drainage.** Is there a sewer, or will each house need its own septic system? Check whether any part of the estate floods after heavy rain.
- **Who pays.** Ask whether services are included in the price, charged separately, or left to you. Get the answer in writing.

For each service, write down one of three words: **done**, **under way** or **planned**. For anything planned, ask for the date in writing, and treat it as a hope until it is done. Recent, dated photos of the site help you see what is really there.

## 4. What can you build on it?

Two plots of the same size can allow very different houses. Rules come from the local authority and, on many estates, from the developer too.

- **Zoning or land use.** Ask what the plot is zoned for: a house, several houses, a business, or something else. If you plan to build more than one home or run a business, ask before you buy.
- **Building lines (setbacks).** How far from each boundary you must build. On a corner plot, ask about both road sides.
- **Coverage and height.** How much of the plot a building may cover, and how many storeys are allowed.
- **Estate rules.** Some estates set a minimum house size, a style, materials, wall heights or a deadline to start building. Ask for the rules as a document, and read them before you sign.
- **Levies.** Ask whether there is a monthly fee to an estate association, what it pays for, and how much it is today.
- **Approvals.** Ask what you need before you can build, such as approved building plans, and who approves them.

Your planner, architect or lawyer can confirm the rules for the plot you choose. If the seller can't tell you what you may build, ask the local authority.

## 5. Are the price and payment terms in writing?

Prices and payment plans are where small misunderstandings become big ones. Get every number on paper before you pay anything.

- **The full price.** Ask whether it includes any tax, and whether there are other costs on top, such as transfer fees, legal fees or service charges.
- **The deposit.** How much, when it is due, and where it goes. Ask whether it is held in a lawyer's trust account or an escrow account until the sale goes through, and what happens to it if the sale falls through.
- **Reservation terms.** If you reserve a plot, how long the reservation lasts, and whether you get your money back if you don't go ahead.
- **Payment plans.** If you pay over time, ask for the deposit, fees, number of months, monthly payment, any interest and the total you will pay. Compare the total with the cash price.
- **Missed payments.** Ask what happens if you miss a payment: a penalty, a grace period, or losing the plot and what you have paid.
- **When the land becomes yours.** Ask at which point transfer happens, and which document proves it.

Everything you agree should be in the signed agreement, with the same numbers as the price list. Have your lawyer or conveyancer read the agreement before you sign it, and pay only into an account the agreement names. Be wary of a request to pay a different account, or of pressure to pay today.

## Your checklist

Before you pay a deposit, check that:

1. You have seen the ownership document, and the owner and seller match.
2. You know whether the plot has its own title, and when it will be in your name.
3. A lawyer or conveyancer has checked the official register.
4. You have the full, approved layout plan.
5. The plot number, size and boundaries are the same on the plan, the price list and the agreement.
6. The land is surveyed and pegged, and you or someone you trust has seen the pegs.
7. You know of every servitude on the plot, in writing.
8. The plot has road access, and you know who maintains the road.
9. Each service is marked done, under way or planned, with dates in writing.
10. You know what the zoning and estate rules let you build.
11. The price, deposit, fees and payment plan are written down, with a total.
12. Your lawyer or conveyancer has read the agreement before you sign.

## For developers: make the checks easy

Buyers who can check quickly decide sooner. If you sell plots, have the answers ready before they ask: the ownership papers, the approved plan, the survey, the state of each service with a date, the building rules, and one price list with your payment plans worked out in full. Share the same answers with every agent, so buyers hear the same thing from everyone.

## In short

**What should I check before buying a plot?**
Who owns the land and whether they can sell it, that the plot on the plan matches the land and its pegs, that roads and services reach it, what you may build, and that the price and payment terms are in writing.

**Who should check the documents?**
A lawyer or conveyancer for the title, the register and the agreement. A land surveyor for the plan and the pegs on site.

**Should I pay a deposit before the checks are done?**
It is safer not to. Ask where the deposit is held and when you get it back, and have the agreement checked before you sign and pay.

**What if I can't visit the plot?**
Ask for the full plan, recent dated photos, and someone you trust to see the pegs. An interactive site plan helps you understand the layout, but it doesn't replace a survey or a legal check.
