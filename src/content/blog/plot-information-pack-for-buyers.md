A plot information pack should let a buyer understand one plot without a site visit or a phone call. Put in nine things: which plot it is and when the pack was made, a site plan with every boundary length, the plot's key facts, labelled pictures, the full price and payment plans, the state of roads and services, which ownership papers exist, what the rules let you build, and who to talk to next. Keep it short, date it, and make every number match your price list.

## What a plot information pack is for

A plot information pack (some call it a property information pack for buyers) is the document a buyer keeps after they first ask about a plot. It answers the questions they would otherwise ask you one by one, and it travels: buyers forward it to a partner, a parent abroad, a lawyer or a bank.

That last part matters most. The person who decides is often not the person who enquired. If the pack is clear on its own, it does your selling for you in conversations you never see. If it is vague, every unanswered question becomes a delay.

A good pack is one plot, not the whole estate. A general brochure sells the place; the plot pack helps someone decide on one piece of it.

The list below works for any estate, wherever it is. Some markets call a plot a stand, a lot or an erf; the contents don't change.

## 1. Which plot, and when

Start with the basics, at the top of the first page:

- **The estate's name and the plot number**, written the same way as on the plan and the price list.
- **The status**: for sale, reserved or sold.
- **The developer's name and logo**, so the pack is clearly yours when it is forwarded.
- **The date the pack was made.** Prices and availability change. A date tells the reader how fresh the information is, and stops an old pack from being taken as today's offer.

## 2. A site plan with every boundary

The site plan is what buyers need first, so give it the most space.

- **Show the plot in its place**: its neighbours, the roads it touches and any open space next to it.
- **Write the length of every side in metres**, on the plan itself. Buyers check the size against the sides, and a lawyer will compare them with the survey.
- **Say what each side borders**: a road (and its width), another plot (by number) or open space.
- **Say where the plan comes from**, such as the surveyed general plan, and whether it is approved.

If your buyers are new to plans, our guide on [how to read a layout plan before you buy a plot](/blog/how-to-read-a-layout-plan-before-you-buy-a-plot) explains each part in plain words. It is worth linking from the pack.

## 3. The plot's key facts

A small set of facts, in the same order on every pack, so buyers can compare plots:

- **Size**, in square metres, as surveyed.
- **Street frontage**: the length of the side that faces the road.
- **Road width** of that road.
- **Position**: a corner plot or a mid-block plot. Corner plots have two road sides, which affects building lines and often the price.

Four or five facts are enough. Put them in a box or a short table, not in a paragraph.

## 4. Pictures, labelled honestly

Pictures help a buyer who can't visit imagine the plot. They also cause trouble when they promise more than the plot is.

- **Show the plot from where a person would stand**: from the street, from above, and from the back.
- **Label what each picture is.** A drawing or 3D view is an illustration, not a photo of the site. Say so on the page.
- **Label any house you show.** If a picture shows a house on the plot, say it is an idea to help the buyer picture it, not a design you are selling, unless it is.
- **Date any site photos.** A recent, dated photo of the pegs or the road is worth more than a beautiful old one.

## 5. The full price and payment plans

This is the page buyers read most carefully, and the one most likely to cause a dispute later. Write every number down.

- **The cash price**, and the price per square metre.
- **Every fee on top**, such as an agreement fee or an admin fee, and the total.
- **Each payment plan in full**: the deposit, what is paid upfront, the number of months, the monthly payment, the interest rate and the total paid over the plan. Buyers compare the total with the cash price, so show both.
- **What the prices include and exclude**, such as tax, transfer costs and service connections.
- **A line saying prices may change**, with the date they were correct and a reminder to confirm before paying.

The numbers in the pack must match your price list and the agreement exactly. If they don't, the buyer's lawyer will ask why, and they will be right to.

## 6. Roads and services

Buyers want to know what is there today and what is promised.

For each of road access, water, power, sewer and drainage, write one of three words: **done**, **under way** or **planned**. For anything planned, give a date, and say who pays for the connection. If it isn't decided yet, say that too. An honest "not yet decided" builds more trust than a confident date you later miss.

## 7. Ownership papers

You don't need to attach the title deed to every pack. You do need to tell the buyer what exists and how they can check it:

- **Who owns the land** and how the seller is connected to the owner.
- **Whether the plot has its own title yet**, or when it will, if the estate is still being subdivided.
- **Which documents a buyer's lawyer can see**, and how to ask for them: the title, the approved plan, the survey.

A short list here saves days of back-and-forth once a buyer is serious.

## 8. What the rules let you build

Two plots of the same size can allow very different houses. List the rules that apply, or attach them:

- **Zoning or land use**: one house, several, or something else.
- **Building lines**, coverage and height limits.
- **Estate rules**, such as a minimum house size, a style, wall heights or a deadline to start building.
- **Any monthly levy** to an estate association, and what it pays for.

Where a rule comes from the local authority, say so and suggest the buyer confirms it with their planner or architect.

## 9. Who to talk to next

End with a person, not a form.

- **A name, a phone number and an email address.** If the buyer came through an estate agent, put the agent's details, so the enquiry reaches the person who is helping them. Our post on [how estate agents can share plots so the enquiry reaches them](/blog/share-plots-with-estate-agents) covers this.
- **The next step**, in one line: how to reserve, or how to book a visit.
- **A link to the latest version**, so a reader with an old pack can check today's price and status.

## What to keep out

A pack is not the place for promises you can't keep.

- **No guarantees about value**, such as "prices will double".
- **No services or roads described as finished when they are planned.**
- **No pictures without labels**, and no stock photos presented as your estate.
- **No numbers that differ from the price list.**

Keep it short. Three or four A4 pages is plenty for one plot. A buyer should be able to read it on a phone.

## A checklist for your plot pack

Before you send a plot information pack, check that it has:

1. The estate's name, the plot number, its status and the date the pack was made.
2. A site plan with the length of every side, and what each side borders.
3. The size, street frontage, road width and position (corner or mid-block).
4. Pictures, each labelled as a photo (with a date) or an illustration.
5. The cash price, fees, every payment plan in full, and the total for each.
6. What the price includes and excludes, and a note that prices may change.
7. The state of each service: done, under way or planned, with dates.
8. Which ownership papers exist and how a buyer's lawyer can see them.
9. The rules on what can be built, or where to find them.
10. A named person to contact, the next step, and a link to the latest version.

## In short

**What is a plot information pack?**
A short document about one plot that a buyer can keep and share: where the plot is, its boundaries and facts, its price and payment plans, the state of services, the papers and rules, and who to contact.

**How long should it be?**
Three or four A4 pages for one plot is enough. Put the site plan and the price first; those are what buyers look for.

**Should the pack include the title deed?**
Usually not. Say which papers exist and how a buyer's lawyer can see them.

**How do I keep plot packs up to date?**
Date every pack, keep the numbers the same as your price list, and include a link to the latest version. Alfa Maps makes each pack when the buyer downloads it, so it shows the price and status at that moment.
